The grid is not a network. It is a negotiated agreement between physics, capital, and a handful of regulators who do not always agree with one another. When the agreement holds, a small town five hundred kilometres from the nearest substation gets electricity at the same price as a refinery two blocks from the generator. When the agreement breaks, you find out which town the provincial transmission planner was thinking about last year and which one they were not.

## The map is the policy

The transmission map is the policy document. It is the thing that tells you, before any minister has spoken, where economic activity will be allowed to grow over the next twenty years. The lines are drawn on the basis of expected load, expected generation, and the political weight of the communities involved. Changing them is expensive in money and in time — a five-hundred-kilovolt line is a generation-long commitment.

## What this essay is about

What follows is an attempt to read the Alberta and Western Canadian transmission system as a geographic document — to put it next to the watershed map, the population map, and the industrial-emissions map and ask which of those three is doing the most work. The answer, more often than not, is the watershed map: the places where it is easy to build large hydro or where cooling water is reliable.

> "We make the lines, and then the lines make us." — _Terje Tvedt, paraphrased_

## The capacity market

Most Canadian provinces have moved to some form of capacity market in the last fifteen years. The mechanics differ — Alberta is the obvious outlier — but the underlying logic is the same: pay generators a small premium to be available, on top of the energy they actually deliver. The capacity market is, in effect, a subsidy for redundancy.

The geographic implication is that you are paying to keep generation near the load. A coal plant fifty kilometres from a city is structurally more valuable than a wind farm three hundred kilometres away, even if the wind farm produces more energy per dollar of capital. The capacity market is a tax on distance.
